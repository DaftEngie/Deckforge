# DeckForge wins on latency, not on Wilds

The evidence supports DeckForge's basic shape but not three of its key bets. The shape is a measure-search-verify loop that starts with safe, reversible settings, paces games at 45 frames per second on the OLED's 90 Hz screen, and never counts frame generation as "native". **No measured source shows Monster Hunter Wilds or Space Marine 2 anywhere near a locked native 45 fps on a Deck.** Wilds is "roughly playable" at about 30 fps even after its January 2026 performance patch. Space Marine 2 ran 20–30 fps at launch with dips into the low 20s, and its anti-cheat restricts DeckForge to safe-tier settings. **Frame generation from a 45 fps base cannot be called native under a no-added-latency goal.** Desktop lab measurements at a similar frame rate show it adding roughly 40–75 ms compared with native play using latency reduction, and at least 22 ms even in the most favorable comparison. **Shader rewriting is not a realistic core feature.** The only official way to swap in a replacement shader is a developer debug switch that works only in DirectX 12 games. Nobody has published a measured gain from it on the Deck's graphics driver, and every game or Proton update breaks the work. The strongest levers are elsewhere. For latency it is the frame limiter (the setting that caps frames per second): a 2022 camera test found Steam's built-in cap added 44–87 ms of input lag while a game's own cap added almost nothing, and nobody has re-measured it on current SteamOS. For image quality it is FSR 4, an AI upscaler that costs about 4.5 ms of the 22.2 ms each frame gets at 45 fps (one user's measurement; 6 ms is plausible) but removes the shimmer cheaper upscalers leave. For hardware, undervolting and power tuning are worth single-digit percentages. For the two PS5-era test games, GeForce NOW's Steam Deck app (US$19.99 or MXN 439 a month for its top tier) likely beats every local technique on render settings and battery life, though how its compressed stream looks on the 800p screen has not been measured. Its latency from Mexico City has never been measured, so a US$7.99 day pass should come before any more engineering on Wilds.

## Five parts of the plan hold, and five need changing

Read against the seven overnight notes, the plan in `docs/QUALITY_TARGET.md` and `CLAUDE.md` splits into parts the evidence backs and parts it undercuts. The table is the scorecard; the sections that follow carry the evidence and sources.

| Plan element | Verdict | Why, in one line |
|---|---|---|
| 45 fps paced on the OLED's 90 Hz panel | **Supported** | 45 divides 90 exactly, so every frame shows for two refreshes; 90 Hz also measured far snappier than the LCD |
| A locked native 45 in Wilds and Space Marine 2 | **Contradicted** | Best Deck reports found: about 30 fps (Wilds, 2026) and 20–30 fps (Space Marine 2, 2024, author-tested, no data published); no measured source shows 45 |
| Frame generation tracked separately, never "native" | **Supported, and should go further** | Near a 45 base it adds roughly 40–75 ms (at least 22 ms in the best case); AMD recommends a 60 fps base |
| "No added latency" at a 45 fps cap | **Unproven** | Steam's limiter added 44–87 ms in 2022; untested on current SteamOS |
| Reconstruction (upscaling) as the top visual lever | **Supported, with a cost** | FSR 4 costs about 4.5 ms at 800p and pays off only when the GPU, not the CPU, is the bottleneck |
| `PROTON_FSR4_UPGRADE` as the FSR 4 route | **Outdated** | It belongs to third-party Proton builds and changed meaning in July 2026 |
| Shader-cache warming | **Supported, as measurement discipline** | DeckForge's own driver tweaks can invalidate Steam's pre-compiled shader cache |
| Shader rewriting (later phase) | **Contradicted as a core feature** | Debug-only hooks, no measured gains, breaks with every game or Proton update |
| Tier S = "in-game settings files" | **Needs splitting** | Wilds keeps its settings file in the install folder, which Steam "verify" and updates overwrite |
| Image quality scored by FLIP plus a temporal (video) metric, calibrated by Fede's blind A/B | **Supported** | FLIP (an image-difference score) misses flicker; video metrics built for upscaler and frame-generation artifacts now exist; no published score threshold maps to "visible on a 7-inch screen" |

The pattern behind these verdicts matters more than any single row. **The plan treats frame rate as the hard constraint and assumes latency comes free. The evidence suggests the reverse.** On PS5-era games, frame rate is mostly fixed by the hardware and the developer. Input latency is shaped by settings a safe-tier tool controls in every game, anti-cheat titles included.

## Neither test game has been shown near a native 45

The 45 fps target is well chosen for the OLED. Its panel runs anywhere from 45 to 90 Hz but has no variable refresh rate (VRR, where the screen waits for each new frame), so smooth motion depends on every frame landing on a whole number of refreshes ([Eurogamer via Overclockers.ru](https://overclockers.ru/blog/NOVO_GEEK/show/158278/Moe-mnenie-o-Steam-Deck-OLED-Eurogamer)). gamescope, SteamOS's compositor (the program that puts every game frame on screen), enforces a frame cap by dividing the refresh rate by a whole number and holding the game back until the next allowed refresh ([gamescope source](https://github.com/ValveSoftware/gamescope)). **At 45 fps on a 90 Hz panel the divisor is exactly 2, so every frame shows for two 11.1 ms refreshes, with no judder.** The same code hides a trap for a tool. If the refresh is left at 60 Hz with Steam's combined refresh/frame-limit slider turned off, 60 ÷ 45 rounds down to 1 and the 45 cap never engages (inference from the code). DeckForge must therefore always set refresh rate and cap together. The OLED's high refresh also buys responsiveness. Digital Foundry's lab measurements found the OLED 26–33 ms quicker from button to screen than the LCD model in Doom Eternal and Crysis 3, a gap that vanished at 25 Hz. The article does not state the exact cap used on each unit ([PCGamesN](https://www.pcgamesn.com/steam-deck/oled-input-lag)).

Below 45, the panel offers a clean fallback ladder that the current plan does not use. At a 40 fps cap the OLED drops to 80 Hz and shows each frame twice; at 30 fps it stays at 90 Hz and shows each frame three times ([Eurogamer via Overclockers.ru](https://overclockers.ru/blog/NOVO_GEEK/show/158278/Moe-mnenie-o-Steam-Deck-OLED-Eurogamer)). gamescope deliberately picks the highest refresh that is an exact multiple of the target ([gamescope source](https://github.com/ValveSoftware/gamescope)). That gives three evenly paced budgets: **22.2 ms (45 fps at 90 Hz), 25 ms (40 at 80 Hz) and 33.3 ms (30 at 90 Hz)**. A 40 fps cap on a 90 Hz panel without the refresh change would judder, because 90 is not a multiple of 40.

Monster Hunter Wilds is the hardest case. The viral `config.ini` "fixes" of 2025 (a misspelled "Resoltuion" key, thread-priority lines) were never measured. The typo also exists in the game's code, so fixing it "may change nothing" ([Icy Veins](https://www.icy-veins.com/monster-hunter-wilds/news/monster-hunter-wilds-performance-optimization-fps-boost/)). The most thorough Deck bundle, "The Little Engine that Shouldn't", combined below-menu config edits with REFramework scripts (REFramework is a scripting mod that runs inside Capcom's RE Engine games). The scripts switch off global illumination, volumetric fog and wind simulation and push foliage detail down. The bundle claimed about 30–45 fps at reduced resolutions, and its author now says to delete it because official patches made it unnecessary ([Nexus mod 183](https://www.nexusmods.com/monsterhunterwilds/mods/183)). After Capcom's January 2026 patch, Wilds is "roughly playable" at about 30 fps on a Deck with FSR or XeSS upscaling and no frame generation ([Notebookcheck](https://www.notebookcheck.net/Monster-Hunter-Wilds-fixes-DLC-bug-greatly-reduces-FPS-drops-and-runs-at-30-FPS-on-Steam-Deck.1215270.0.html)). Claims of 40+ fps are unmeasured forum posts ([Steam discussion](https://steamcommunity.com/app/2246340/discussions/0/596271270005369411)) or guides that contradict themselves ([EveZone](https://evezone.evetech.co.za/performance-pulse/steam-deck-40fps-settings-mh-wilds)). The mod levers also cost something visible: turning off global illumination and fog broke cutscenes until the mod learned to re-enable them for cinematics ([Nexus mod 234](https://www.nexusmods.com/monsterhunterwilds/mods/234)). **On this evidence a locked native 45 in Wilds is beyond anything reported, even with mods; an honest target is 30–40 fps native (inference).** QUALITY_TARGET.md itself lists Wilds as heavy on both CPU and GPU, and its own back-of-envelope PS5 comparison "breaks for CPU-bound games".

Space Marine 2 offers even less room. It ships Easy Anti-Cheat ([Steam discussion](https://steamcommunity.com/app/2183900/discussions/0/4763207778563513263)), so DeckForge's rules allow only Tier S, and no source documents any setting beyond the in-game menu. Its user data lives in a per-user folder (a save-data path; whether its graphics settings live there too is unverified). Under Proton (Valve's Windows-compatibility layer), that folder sits inside the game's "prefix", a directory that acts as the game's private Windows C: drive ([Prima Games](https://primagames.com/?p=279357); [GamingOnLinux guide](https://www.gamingonlinux.com/guides/view/how-to-find-where-steam-stores-proton-game-files/)). The best Deck report found ran it at 800p with FSR 2, Low textures and shadows, screen-space reflections off and a 15 W power limit, and got roughly 20–30 fps, dipping into the low 20s in big battles ([The Backdash](https://www.thebackdash.com/tech/space-marine-2-steam-deck-settings)). That test dates from September 2024. A December 2024 patch added FSR 3 and frame generation ([GamingOnLinux](https://www.gamingonlinux.com/2024/12/space-marine-2-adds-dlss3-fsr3-and-frame-generation-more-playable-on-steam-deck-needs-launch-option-for-desktop-linux/page=1/)), and no measured Deck result after that patch was found. **The expected honest result for Space Marine 2 is "BLOCKED at around 30 fps in hordes" (inference).** Its built-in frame generation is the only route to a higher number on screen. Helldivers 2, the later safe-tier title, runs on Proton after an April 2026 hotfix ([GamingOnLinux](https://www.gamingonlinux.com/2026/04/proton-hotfix-updated-to-fix-helldivers-2-on-linux-steamos-systems/page=1/)).

None of this makes Wilds a bad test case; it makes it the wrong *only* case. Earlier DeckForge research split the problem in two. Some games waste the hardware they have, which a tool can fix. Others were built for hardware about five times stronger, a gap no software closes. Both current test games sit in the second group. Valve's own bar is lower than DeckForge's: a "Verified" game need only reach 30 fps at 800p on its default settings ([Steamworks](https://partner.steamgames.com/doc/steamhardware/compat)). DeckForge needs at least one test game whose untuned frame time is already within roughly 20–30% of the 22.2 ms budget, so the loop can show a real win (recommendation). It also needs a first-class "BLOCKED, with evidence" verdict for the rest, which is exactly what rule R1 asks for.

## The frame limiter, not the upscaler, sets input latency

Input latency is the delay between pressing a button and seeing the result. It is the part of Fede's goal the plan treats as free, and the evidence says it is not. On the Deck the chain runs from the controller through the game's simulation into a render queue. By default, DirectX lets the CPU prepare up to three frames ahead of the GPU ([Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/win32/api/dxgi/nf-dxgi-idxgidevice1-setmaximumframelatency)). Next come GPU rendering and the translation layer that converts DirectX calls to Vulkan (DXVK for DirectX 9–11, vkd3d-proton for DirectX 12, both inside Proton). Finally gamescope hands the frame to the panel. **Digital Foundry measured 51–65 ms button-to-screen for real games on the OLED** ([PCGamesN](https://www.pcgamesn.com/steam-deck/oled-input-lag)). That is the right reference for "native feel" on this device.

The biggest measured swing in that chain is the frame limiter. In 2022 a tester filmed a Deck LCD at 240 fps in a simple 2D game. Latency went from 31.8 ms uncapped to 75.8 ms with Steam's system cap at 60 fps, and to 121.1 ms with a 40 Hz panel and 40 fps cap. Rocket League's own in-game limiter added "minimal to zero" delay ([Tom's Hardware](https://www.tomshardware.com/uk/news/steam-deck-framerate-limiter-egregiously-raises-input-latency)). The code shows why this can happen. gamescope enforces the cap by withholding the game's "frame done" signal until the next allowed refresh ([gamescope source](https://github.com/ValveSoftware/gamescope)), so the game waits *after* it has already read the controller. That is the classic way a limiter adds lag (inference). The 2022 test predates the OLED, the combined refresh/frame-limit slider, and a `GAMESCOPE_LOW_LATENCY` control that now exists in gamescope's code with no documentation of what it does. No 2023–2026 re-measurement was found. **This is the single most important unknown for a "locked 45, no added latency" product.** It also pulls against smoothness. DXVK's documentation says its own limiter should not be used when gamescope's is available, because gamescope's paces frames better ([dxvk.conf](https://github.com/doitsujin/dxvk/blob/master/dxvk.conf)). Steam Deck HQ, meanwhile, advises turning the SteamOS cap off and using the game's vsync for the lowest lag ([SDHQ](https://steamdeckhq.com/tips-and-guides/the-sdhq-performance-settings-encyclopedia/)). DeckForge cannot choose a limiter strategy until it has measured these options against each other at 45 fps on the 90 Hz panel.

The second lever is queue depth. When the GPU is the bottleneck, frames pile up in the render queue, and each extra queued frame at 45 fps can add up to 22 ms (inference). For DirectX 9–11 games, DXVK's `dxgi.maxFrameLatency` setting shortens that queue. Its `dxvk.latencySleep` adds a built-in latency limiter, though that does nothing when an external limiter is active ([dxvk.conf](https://github.com/doitsujin/dxvk/blob/master/dxvk.conf)). Both can be passed per game in launch options, so both are Tier S. For DirectX 12 games such as Wilds, which runs through vkd3d-proton ([GamingOnLinux](https://www.gamingonlinux.com/2026/05/vkd3d-proton-3-0-1-brings-many-linux-gaming-enhancements-for-direct3d-12-via-vulkan/)), current vkd3d-proton documents no equivalent queue setting ([vkd3d-proton README](https://github.com/HansKristian-Work/vkd3d-proton/blob/master/README.md)).

The third lever is a Reflex-style limiter. NVIDIA Reflex and AMD Anti-Lag 2 time the CPU so it starts each frame just before the GPU needs it, keeping the queue empty. On desktop PCs, PCGH's lab measurements found these cut 21–22 ms from GPU-bound games running at 41–76 fps ([PCGH](https://www.pcgameshardware.de/Starfield-Spiel-61756/Specials/Lossless-Scaling-Test-Frame-Generation-FSR-3-DLSS-Latenz-LSFG-1446068/2/)). That is exactly the Deck's situation in demanding games. On Linux the credible implementation is low_latency_layer (MIT-licensed, May 2026). It is an opt-in Vulkan layer (a plug-in that sits between the game and the graphics driver) implementing both AMD's and NVIDIA's interfaces. Its author's desktop measurements show it matching or beating Windows Anti-Lag 2, while Mesa's own anti-lag layer measured as a no-op ([low_latency_layer README](https://github.com/Korthos-Software/low_latency_layer); [Phoronix](https://phoronix.com/news/Low-Latency-Layer)). It has never been tested on a Deck, and as an injected layer it is Tier I. Its NVIDIA-spoofing options are documented to break Proton's FSR 4 upgrade path, so some games will force a choice between better upscaling and lower latency. The older alternative, LatencyFleX, "does not provide any benefits when VSync is enabled" ([LatencyFleX](https://github.com/ishitatsuyuki/LatencyFleX)). The Deck's game mode forces vsync unless "Allow Tearing" is switched on ([GamingOnLinux](https://gamingonlinux.com/2022/10/vrr-external-screens-and-allow-tearing-options-coming-to-steam-deck)). Steam Deck HQ says that option only matters on external displays ([SDHQ](https://steamdeckhq.com/tips-and-guides/the-sdhq-performance-settings-encyclopedia/)), so whether it does anything on the built-in screen is unresolved.

Frame generation (FG) creates in-between frames by blending two real ones, so it must hold back the newest real frame before showing anything. PCGH's lab measurements of Starfield at about 41 fps are the closest published analogue to a 45 fps Deck, and they show the cost ([PCGH](https://www.pcgameshardware.de/Starfield-Spiel-61756/Specials/Lossless-Scaling-Test-Frame-Generation-FSR-3-DLSS-Latenz-LSFG-1446068/2/)):

| Starfield, ~41 fps real frames (desktop GTX 1080 Ti) | Button-to-screen | Versus native with Reflex |
|---|---|---|
| Native, Reflex on | 72 ms | baseline |
| Native, Reflex off | 93 ms | +21 ms |
| Lossless Scaling FG + Reflex | 115 ms | +43 ms |
| Lossless Scaling FG | 140 ms | +68 ms |
| FSR 3 FG + Reflex | 145 ms | +73 ms |
| FSR 3 FG | 150 ms | +78 ms |

**Even the best frame-generation result is 43 ms slower than native with latency reduction, and 22 ms slower than native without it.** Across PCGH's tests, FG added one to three real frames of delay. At a 45 fps base that is an estimated +22 to +65 ms, most likely around +40–45 ms (inference), which would put a heavy game near 95–140 ms in total against the OLED's measured 51–65 ms native. AMD says its frame generation "runs best" from at least 60 fps and should be avoided below 30 ([AMD GPUOpen](https://gpuopen.com/fidelityfx-super-resolution-3/)). TechPowerUp found that 30 fps generated up to 60 felt like about 35 fps ([TechPowerUp](https://www.techpowerup.com/review/forspoken-fsr-3/)). Lossless Scaling also cannot separate the game's HUD from the scene, so menus and health bars ghost. In one of PCGH's desktop comparisons, the game rendered about 10% fewer real frames with it running ([PCGH](https://www.pcgameshardware.de/Starfield-Spiel-61756/Specials/Lossless-Scaling-Test-Frame-Generation-FSR-3-DLSS-Latenz-LSFG-1446068/)). On a Deck whose GPU is already saturated, that can drag the real frame rate below 45. Steam Deck HQ found the lsfg-vk 2.0 plugin did "not improve image quality", brought "no frame-pacing improvements", and still had a felt delay ([SDHQ](https://steamdeckhq.com/news/lossless-scaling-2-0-doesnt-improve-frame-gen/)).

**The plan is right to keep frame generation out of the native number, and it should go further.** FG at a 45 base fails Fede's no-added-latency rule by construction. It belongs in the product only as an opt-in "motion smoothing" mode that displays its measured latency cost. Two more constraints apply. lsfg-vk loads into the game process as a Vulkan layer, so by the same logic that puts DeckForge's own layer in Tier I, it is Tier I too. The research turned up no reported Linux ban tied to it, but no reports is not proof of safety. lsfg-vk also moved to a CC BY-NC-ND licence in August 2026 ([GamingOnLinux](https://www.gamingonlinux.com/2026/08/lossless-scaling-frame-generation-on-linux-lsfg-vk-moves-away-from-github-changes-license-due-to-ai-slop-forks/)), so DeckForge, an MIT project, can detect and launch a user's own copy but cannot bundle or modify it.

What should "native feel" mean in practice? No study found sets a just-noticeable latency difference for gamepad play on a handheld. NVIDIA research found that above 60 Hz, cutting latency helped aiming tasks more than raising the refresh rate ([NVIDIA Research](https://research.nvidia.com/publication/2019-11_Latency-of-30)). A defensible, testable rule (a judgment, not an established constant) has two parts. A configuration counts as native if its median button-to-screen latency is within 5 ms of the best native-45 configuration for that game, roughly the noise of a phone-camera test. Anything adding 20 ms or more, about one 45 fps frame, is not native. This needs no lab gear. In the 2022 Deck study, a phone filming at 240 fps, averaged over repeated presses, resolved differences to about ±4 ms ([Tom's Hardware](https://www.tomshardware.com/uk/news/steam-deck-framerate-limiter-egregiously-raises-input-latency)).

## FSR 4 INT8 buys the most image quality per millisecond

Reconstruction remains the top image-quality lever. It means rendering fewer pixels and letting a temporal upscaler (one that reuses detail from previous frames) rebuild a sharp 800p image, and the evidence names a clear leader. FSR 4 is AMD's machine-learning upscaler. The Deck's RDNA 2 GPU lacks the 8-bit floating-point (FP8) hardware FSR 4 was designed for, so it runs a slower 8-bit integer (INT8) version ([TechSpot](https://www.techspot.com/news/112412-fsr-4-upscaling-finally-coming-older-radeon-gpus.html)). AMD has promised official RDNA 2 support in "early 2027" ([GamingOnLinux](https://www.gamingonlinux.com/2026/05/amd-announce-fsr-upscaling-4-1-officially-coming-to-rdna-3-and-rdna-2/)). It has also promised a "lighter" handheld version by the end of 2026, but did not name the Deck ([SDHQ](https://steamdeckhq.com/news/amd-fsr-4-confirmed-for-handhelds-2026/); [Notebookcheck](https://www.notebookcheck.net/FSR-4-special-version-to-boost-gaming-performance-on-handhelds-coming-this-year.1418417.0.html)). Unofficially it works today. OptiScaler 0.9.4's `Fsr4ForceEnableInt8` switch runs FSR 4.1.1 INT8 on the Deck ([Decky Framegen issue #204](https://github.com/xXJSONDeruloXx/Decky-Framegen/issues/204)). The third-party Proton-CachyOS build automatically upgrades games that ship FSR 3.1 ([GamingOnLinux](https://www.gamingonlinux.com/2026/07/proton-cachyos-version-11-0-20260702-brings-amd-fsr4-upgrades-pipewire-changes/)). Valve's stock Proton has no announced RDNA 2 path; an FSR 4.1.1 INT8 file briefly appeared in a Proton Experimental download and was withdrawn ([igor'sLAB](https://www.igorslab.de/en/valve-fsr-4-1-proton-experimental-rdna-3-3-5/)).

That makes the route named in QUALITY_TARGET.md out of date. `PROTON_FSR4_UPGRADE` belongs to third-party Proton builds, and Proton-EM's documentation warned that RDNA 2 performance would be "pretty terrible" ([Proton-EM FSR4.md](https://github.com/Etaash-mathamsetty/Proton/blob/em-10-hdrtest/docs/FSR4.md)). In Proton-CachyOS, `PROTON_FSR4_UPGRADE` now only requests a specific FSR 4 version and a new `FSR4_UPGRADE` variable performs the upgrade. The release notes say the new variable is not needed on discrete RDNA 2–4 cards, which makes it the relevant switch for an integrated GPU like the Deck's, and one commenter reported about 25% lower performance on an integrated GPU ([GamingOnLinux](https://www.gamingonlinux.com/2026/07/proton-cachyos-version-11-0-20260702-brings-amd-fsr4-upgrades-pipewire-changes/)). Switching Proton builds is itself a risky knob: a GamingOnLinux reader reported a Denuvo lockout after changing Proton version, and an admin replied that such lockouts last 24 hours (an unverified user report, [GamingOnLinux](https://www.gamingonlinux.com/guides/view/anticheat-check-which-competitive-games-actually-work-on-linux-steamos/)). One Deck outlet wrote that FSR 4 uses FP8 on the Deck ([SDHQ](https://steamdeckhq.com/?p=50571)). That contradicts AMD's statement that older cards use INT8, and is almost certainly wrong.

**FSR 4's cost on the Deck is where two overnight notes disagree.** It matters because the cost decides whether FSR 4 fits a 45 fps budget at all. The upscaling note cites **about 4.5 ms per frame at 800p, measured by one community member on a Deck with the GPU at 1,400 MHz**; the article does not say which FSR 4 build or code path was used ([Wccftech](https://wccftech.com/helixsr-mod-dlss-3-7-steam-deck-amd-handhelds-faster-fsr-4/)). The latency note instead scales timings from a different RDNA 2 board, the BC-250, by pixel count and compute-unit count, and arrives at 6–11 ms, a quarter to half of the frame. **The 4.5 ms figure is the stronger evidence.** It was taken on the target hardware. Steam Deck HQ's Spider-Man test, which implies FSR 4 cost about 3.2 ms *more than* FSR 3, is consistent with it once FSR 3's own estimated 0.8–1.6 ms is added ([SDHQ](https://steamdeckhq.com/news/we-can-now-use-fsr-4-on-the-steam-deck-for-better-visuals-with-a-power-tradeoff/)). A leaked 2025 INT8 build cost about 4.8 ms more than FSR 3 at 720p, which implies roughly 6 ms in total, closer to the pessimistic estimate ([NoobFeed](https://www.noobfeed.com/hardware/amd-fsr4-int8-upscaling-consoles-gpus)). The 6–11 ms estimate stacks two scaling assumptions on a board whose active compute-unit count in the test was never stated: 24 stock, up to 40 with community firmware ([Guru3D](https://www.guru3d.com/story/optimized-fsr-4-nearly-halves-upscaling-time-on-amd-repurposed-bc250-mining-board/)). It also assumes speed scales perfectly with compute units, which nothing in the notes tests (inference). Treat it as a pessimistic bound. The working figure is therefore **about 4.5 ms, with about 6 ms plausible: roughly 20–27% of the 22.2 ms budget**, until DeckForge's experiment E9 measures it. DeckForge must confirm it with its own GPU timestamps, because the FSR 4 build behind the number is unknown and the Deck's GPU can run up to 1,600 MHz ([GamersNexus](https://gamersnexus.net/news/valve-announces-steam-deck-oled-deck-2-works)). The latency note also used an 88 GB/s memory-bandwidth figure carried over from earlier research. That is the LCD number; the OLED's 6,400 MT/s memory delivers 102.4 GB/s ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steam_Deck)).

FSR 4's cost is set by the output resolution, not the input. On the BC-250, going from 1080p to 4K output (four times the pixels) took about 3.6 times as long ([Guru3D](https://www.guru3d.com/story/optimized-fsr-4-nearly-halves-upscaling-time-on-amd-repurposed-bc250-mining-board/)). Its cost therefore has to be paid back by rendering fewer pixels. Dropping from FSR 3.1's Quality input (853×533) to FSR 4's Performance input (640×400) cuts rendered pixels by about 44%. In a GPU-bound game, that plausibly saves more than FSR 4's estimated 3–3.5 ms premium. On desktop RDNA 3, TechSpot found FSR 4.1 Performance generally looked better than FSR 3.1 Quality, though in Horizon Zero Dawn Remastered it also ran slower ([TechSpot](https://www.techspot.com/article/3142-amd-fsr-4-upscaling-rdna-3/)). **The rule DeckForge should encode: FSR 4 Performance can match FSR 3.1 Quality on frame time while looking better, but only in GPU-bound games whose cost scales with resolution (inference). In CPU-bound scenes its fixed cost is pure loss.** That is why diagnosis must come before any upscaler search, and why Wilds is a poor showcase for FSR 4.

The quality evidence is consistent. On a Deck, FSR 4 removed the "very pixelated, shiny" sparkle of FSR 3 and sharpened distant windows ([SDHQ](https://steamdeckhq.com/news/we-can-now-use-fsr-4-on-the-steam-deck-for-better-visuals-with-a-power-tradeoff/)). It was better than FSR 3.1 in every title NoobFeed tested, with steadier edges and less breakup in motion ([NoobFeed](https://www.noobfeed.com/hardware/fsr4-steam-deck-tested-aaa-games)). The caveats are real. An INT8-specific ghosting problem on RDNA 2 was "significantly" reduced in March 2026 but not eliminated ([Tom's Hardware](https://www.tomshardware.com/pc-components/gpus/optiscaler-team-fixes-int8-fsr-4-ghosting-on-rx-6000-series-gpus-adds-support-for-the-latest-adrenalin-drivers)). TechSpot found that INT8 matches FP8 only down to about 850p input, with INT8 "slightly worse" in edge cases at low resolutions ([TechSpot](https://www.techspot.com/article/3142-amd-fsr-4-upscaling-rdna-3/)). The Deck feeds FSR 4 just 400–533 lines, below anything tested, so its quality at Deck resolutions is genuinely unknown. It has to be scored with a temporal metric that catches ghosting, not a still-image one.

Three newer options could shift the balance. A community fork of FSR 4 (4.0.0-rc9) cut its run time on the BC-250 from 7.13 to 3.93 ms at 1080p through integer-math optimizations ([Guru3D](https://www.guru3d.com/story/optimized-fsr-4-nearly-halves-upscaling-time-on-amd-repurposed-bc250-mining-board/)). It has never been tried on a Deck; if the gain carries over, FSR 4 at 800p would cost about 2.3–2.5 ms (inference). That makes porting or tracking this fork the single most valuable upscaling experiment. HelixSR, released in October 2026, runs NVIDIA's DLSS 3.7 model as plain DirectX 12 compute shaders and measured about 3.5 ms at 800p on a Deck, faster than FSR 4 ([Wccftech](https://wccftech.com/helixsr-mod-dlss-3-7-steam-deck-amd-handhelds-faster-fsr-4/)). DLSS model weights are NVIDIA's property and HelixSR's licence was not found, so DeckForge should at most benchmark it privately and never bundle or automate it (inference on licensing). XeSS's DP4a mode is the middle ground. In Red Dead Redemption on a Deck it cost about 15 fps versus basic FXAA anti-aliasing (76 down to 61) and kept more detail than FSR 3, but shimmered on high-contrast edges ([NoobFeed](https://www.noobfeed.com/hardware/red-dead-redemption-steam-deck-xess-fsr4)). In DirectX 11 games, OptiScaler's interop path for it costs up to about 10% ([OptiScaler README](https://github.com/optiscaler/OptiScaler)).

The FSR 3.1 estimate rests on AMD's published desktop timings ([AMD FSR2 README](https://github.com/GPUOpen-Effects/FidelityFX-FSR2)), and the gamescope filters are listed in its documentation and code ([gamescope README](https://github.com/ValveSoftware/gamescope/blob/master/README.md); [gamescope main.cpp](https://github.com/ValveSoftware/gamescope/blob/master/src/main.cpp)).

Two cheaper levers sit around the upscaler. The first is texture LOD bias, which controls how sharp a version of each texture (its "mipmap") the GPU samples. AMD recommends a bias of log2(render ÷ display resolution) − 1, which is −2.0 at Performance mode ([AMD FSR2 README](https://github.com/GPUOpen-Effects/FidelityFX-FSR2)). At 400p-to-800p that can make fine textures shimmer, and sharper mipmaps cost memory bandwidth (inference). DXVK exposes `samplerLodBias` and `clampNegativeLodBias` for DirectX 9–11 games ([dxvk.conf](https://github.com/doitsujin/dxvk/blob/71ec7b5be6c517db39bcca4d38f38a63f6ddbc56/dxvk.conf)). The game-files note could not fetch that file and listed these settings as unverified, but the shader note read it at a pinned commit and confirmed them. vkd3d-proton has no such knob for DirectX 12 ([vkd3d-proton README](https://github.com/HansKristian-Work/vkd3d-proton/blob/2230755878b01993b4b82d0ac0c0624f3ab4d333/README.md)). DirectX 12 games therefore need OptiScaler's mipmap override (Tier I) or DeckForge's planned layer hook.

The second lever is sharpening. gamescope's sharpness runs from 0 (strongest) to 20 (weakest), with a default of 2 ([gamescope main.cpp](https://github.com/ValveSoftware/gamescope/blob/master/src/main.cpp)). OptiScaler's RCAS sharpening defaults to 0.3 ([OptiScaler.ini](https://github.com/optiscaler/OptiScaler/blob/master/OptiScaler.ini)), and the only Deck-specific advice found is 0.25–0.3 ([NoobFeed](https://www.noobfeed.com/hardware/red-dead-redemption-steam-deck-xess-fsr4)). A search range of about 0.2–0.4 is a reasonable start, not an established optimum (inference). gamescope can also apply ReShade post-processing without touching the game, but its README warns this adds latency ([gamescope README](https://github.com/ValveSoftware/gamescope/blob/master/README.md)). Under Fede's goal, that makes it the wrong place for sharpening.

QUALITY_TARGET.md's idea of spending on what a 7-inch screen shows and cutting what it doesn't is also supported, but with a strong warning about placebo. In Oblivion Remastered (Unreal Engine 5) on a Deck, disabling Lumen, UE5's real-time global illumination, through two Engine.ini lines gained 5–10 fps at a modest visual cost ([overkill.wtf](https://overkill.wtf/elder-scrolls-oblivion-remastered-best-settings-steam-deck)). Yet Digital Foundry's A/B test found the popular "Ultimate Engine Tweaks" Engine.ini pack ran "the same" as stock ([VideoGamer](https://www.videogamer.com/?p=1162217)). CD Projekt Red removed Cyberpunk's famous memory-budget file as leftover development clutter ([PCGamesN](https://www.pcgamesn.com/cyberpunk-2077/config-file-fix)), and Wilds' typo fix was debunked. **DeckForge should search a small, curated set of heavy systems (global illumination, shadows, resolution, foliage and view distance). It should confirm each setting actually took effect, through a frame-time change and an image difference, before crediting it.** One check deserves to come first. Valve's vkd3d-proton already switches off ray tracing by default on the Deck for games that turn it on automatically, "leading to very poor performance by default" ([vkd3d-proton device_workarounds.c](https://github.com/HansKristian-Work/vkd3d-proton/blob/2230755878b01993b4b82d0ac0c0624f3ab4d333/libs/vkd3d/device_workarounds.c)). Every game's ray-tracing defaults are worth inspecting first.

## Shader work should mean stutter control, not rewriting

A shader is a small program the GPU runs for every pixel or vertex. Before it can run, the driver must compile it, together with the GPU state it needs, into a "pipeline". When a game asks for a pipeline nobody compiled in advance, the frame waits and the player sees a hitch. The Deck already fights this at four levels. First, Steam pre-compiles each game's pipelines in the background with Valve's Fossilize tool ([GamingOnLinux](https://www.gamingonlinux.com/articles/16761/)); in a Valve developer's words, "On Steam Deck, shaders are pre-compiled" ([Phoronix](https://www.phoronix.com/news/RADV-Force-Shader-Re-Comp)). Second, DXVK and vkd3d-proton keep their own caches of translated shaders. Third, RADV (Mesa's Vulkan driver for AMD GPUs, the Deck's graphics driver) caches compiled GPU code on disk. Fourth, a fast-link path called the graphics pipeline library softens first-time compiles.

What stutter remains is mostly the game engine's doing. DXVK's documentation says games that only load shaders at draw time, "e.g. most Unreal Engine games", will still stutter ([DXVK README](https://github.com/doitsujin/dxvk/blob/71ec7b5be6c517db39bcca4d38f38a63f6ddbc56/README.md)). Epic's own precaching system leaves gaps too, including micro-stutter the first time a material is drawn and separate "traversal" hitches from loading ([Epic tech blog](https://www.unrealengine.com/en-US/tech-blog/game-engines-and-shader-stuttering-unreal-engines-solution-to-the-problem)). These are developer-side problems; no player-side Engine.ini edit fixes them (inference).

The most important finding for DeckForge's design is a trap. Steam's pre-compiled caches are built with default driver settings. Mesa's own per-game configuration file warns that shader-affecting workarounds keyed to a game's executable "aren't applied when replaying with Fossilize for precompilation" ([Mesa 00-radv-defaults.conf](https://github.com/chaotic-cx/mesa-mirror/blob/bffd91947efc1932da99aca69fc540d6d4201b0c/src/amd/vulkan/00-radv-defaults.conf)). Valve even added driver options whose only job is to force a game's pipelines to recompile ([Phoronix](https://www.phoronix.com/news/RADV-Force-Shader-Re-Comp)). **Any DeckForge knob that changes how the driver compiles shaders will likely miss Steam's cache and cause first-run stutter (inference).** That includes wave-size flags, shader-affecting driver options, and possibly forced variable-rate shading. Such settings must be scored on a warm second run, with the first-run cost reported separately.

That makes "shader-cache warming" a measurement discipline more than a feature. The clean test runs the same game segment twice, cold then warm. RADV's `psocachestats` option reports cache hits and misses ([Mesa envvars](https://github.com/chaotic-cx/mesa-mirror/blob/bffd91947efc1932da99aca69fc540d6d4201b0c/docs/envvars.rst)), and MangoHud, an overlay that can log every frame's time, records the run ([MangoHud README](https://github.com/flightlessmango/MangoHud/blob/e5d31119b521775de01ec41154c3ddb0a4c8c0cb/README.md)). Spikes that vanish on the warm run were compile stutter; spikes that remain are streaming, traversal or CPU stalls. Against a 22.2 ms budget, the useful stutter metric counts frames longer than about 44 ms per minute, plus the worst frame. The usual "1% lows" figure blurs a handful of 200 ms hitches into a small number (inference). To warm a cache, DeckForge should ask the user to let Steam finish its shader processing, or run a scripted warm-up. Replaying Steam's Fossilize databases by hand would mean reproducing Steam's exact environment and touching the real Steam install, which rule R7 forbids in development. One untested knob deserves an A/B test: DXVK compiles on all CPU cores by default ([dxvk.conf](https://github.com/doitsujin/dxvk/blob/71ec7b5be6c517db39bcca4d38f38a63f6ddbc56/dxvk.conf)), which competes with the game itself on a 4-core CPU.

Shader *rewriting* is a different matter, and the evidence argues against it as a core feature on three counts. The hooks are debug-only. The only official replace-by-hash hook is vkd3d-proton's `VKD3D_SHADER_OVERRIDE`, documented among features "only meant to be used by vkd3d-proton developers" ([vkd3d-proton README](https://github.com/HansKristian-Work/vkd3d-proton/blob/2230755878b01993b4b82d0ac0c0624f3ab4d333/README.md)). DXVK, which handles DirectX 9–11, can dump shaders but has no replace path at all ([DXVK source](https://github.com/doitsujin/dxvk/tree/71ec7b5be6c517db39bcca4d38f38a63f6ddbc56/src)). Generic optimization adds little. The Deck's production shader compiler, ACO, exposes only debugging switches ([Mesa envvars](https://github.com/chaotic-cx/mesa-mirror/blob/bffd91947efc1932da99aca69fc540d6d4201b0c/docs/envvars.rst)), and generic optimizer passes largely repeat what it already does (inference). Real gains would need meaning-changing edits, such as lower precision or fewer samples in blur, ambient-occlusion and fog shaders, each of which would then need image and temporal validation in every scene. Maintenance breaks it. Replacement shaders are keyed to hashes that change with game patches, a churn that pushed vkd3d-proton 3.0.1 to re-key its own workarounds to entry-point names ([GamingOnLinux](https://www.gamingonlinux.com/2026/05/vkd3d-proton-3-0-1-brings-many-linux-gaming-enhancements-for-direct3d-12-via-vulkan/)). They depend on the translator's output, which changed wholesale when vkd3d-proton 3.0 replaced its shader front end ([GamingOnLinux](https://www.gamingonlinux.com/2025/11/vkd3d-proton-3-0-brings-fsr4-support-shader-backend-rewrite-lots-of-bug-fixes-and-performance-upgrades/page=1/)). And they miss Steam's pre-compiled cache.

**No published measurement of a frame-time gain from replaced shaders on the Deck's driver exists for any commercial game.** Automatic shader simplification has been studied for more than a decade, for example through genetic programming in 2011 ([Sitthi-amorn et al.](https://web.eecs.umich.edu/~weimerw/p/sitthiamorn_siga11.pdf)), without becoming a shipping tool. Earlier DeckForge research attributed that to verification and maintenance, not a lack of cleverness. One thing has changed since: Valve and RADV developers are discussing driver-side per-game shader replacement, still at the "ideas" stage ([Phoronix](https://phoronix.com/news/Mesa-RADV-More-Per-Game-Tuning)). If that lands, a validated rewrite's natural home is upstream in the driver, not in a user-side override. Keep the AI shader track as research. Before automating anything, run the backlog's manual experiment: hand-edit one expensive shader in RenderDoc (a frame-capture and shader-editing tool) and measure what a single rewrite buys.

## The Deck's spare hardware is worth single digits

Squeezing the silicon is worth less than the other levers. The OLED keeps the LCD model's 4-core Zen 2 CPU and 8-compute-unit RDNA 2 GPU (about 1.6 TFLOPS), on a 6 nm process, inside a shared 4–15 W power budget for the APU (the combined CPU-GPU chip) ([GamersNexus](https://gamersnexus.net/news/valve-announces-steam-deck-oled-deck-2-works)). Its faster memory gives 102.4 GB/s instead of 88 GB/s ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steam_Deck)), and reviewers measured it 2–9% faster than the LCD depending on content ([Eurogamer via Overclockers.ru](https://overclockers.ru/blog/NOVO_GEEK/show/158278/Moe-mnenie-o-Steam-Deck-OLED-Eurogamer)). Because CPU and GPU share one budget, extra power helps most when both are busy at once. One community measurement found the OLED at its 15 W limit drew about 23 W for the whole system, against about 27 W for the LCD ([willett.io](https://www.willett.io/posts/watts/)). TDP numbers (the power cap) therefore do not transfer between models.

Valve now provides a clean way in. steamos-manager is Valve's MIT-licensed system service, and its `steamosctl` command exposes TDP, GPU clock and performance level, CPU governor and boost, fan control and the CPU scheduler ([GamingOnLinux](https://gamingonlinux.com/2025/05/steamos-manager-for-bios-updates-tdp-and-gpu-clock-controls-now-open-source); [steamosctl man page](https://linuxcommandlibrary.com/man/steamosctl)). It keeps values within Valve's limits. It also avoids the raw root-level file writes that existing Decky plugins perform with shell commands, a pattern DeckForge's rule R11 forbids ([SimpleDeckyTDP](https://github.com/aarron-lee/SimpleDeckyTDP)). The conflict is Steam itself. Steam stores each game's quick-access performance profile as an undocumented binary blob in `config.vdf` and re-applies it at every launch. The PowerTools plugin's maintainer refused to edit that blob because neither the format nor the API carries any stability guarantee ([PowerTools issue #88](https://github.com/NGnius/PowerTools/issues/88)). Until SteamOS 3.8, those profiles "would intermittently fail to apply" ([GamingOnLinux](https://www.gamingonlinux.com/2026/06/steamos-3-8-is-out-with-initial-steam-machine-support-desktop-mode-upgrades-new-graphics-drivers/page=1/)). The safest path is for DeckForge to recommend values the user sets in Steam's own per-game profile, then check after launch that they took effect.

The remaining knobs are small. Valve's own BIOS undervolt (running the chip at slightly lower voltage) at −30 mV left Witcher 3's frame rate unchanged, cut battery use about 14% at a 45 fps cap, and improved Hitman 3's worst frames ([HotHardware](https://amp.hothardware.com/reviews/steam-deck-undervolting-tested-a-little-extra-battery-life-and-performance)). On an OLED, one user reports +2–4% ([Lemmy](https://lemmy.ndlug.org/u/thubn@discuss.tchncs.de)). Stable settings vary from unit to unit, so undervolting is a step for the user, not something to automate. The GPU stays capped near 1,600 MHz without unsupported BIOS hacks ([Steam Community](https://steamcommunity.com/app/1675200/discussions/1/4330852793642803094)). A 2023 LCD overclock gained 5–9% at 21–23 W, with a risk of bricking the device, and memory overclocking showed "no measurable improvement" ([SDHQ](https://steamdeckhq.com/news/undervolting-and-overclocking-push-your-steam-deck-beyond-its-limits/)). SteamOS 3.8 added LAVD as an opt-in CPU scheduler, the system part that decides which task runs on which core ([GamingOnLinux](https://www.gamingonlinux.com/2026/06/steamos-3-8-is-out-with-initial-steam-machine-support-desktop-mode-upgrades-new-graphics-drivers/page=1/)). It is reported to perform "similar or better" than the default on the Deck ([Phoronix](https://www.phoronix.com/news/Meta-SCX-LAVD-Steam-Deck-Server)), but with no published Deck numbers. That makes it a cheap, reversible A/B test for CPU-bound games like Wilds. SteamOS 3.8.28 reworked video-memory handling, which may make the old BIOS frame-buffer tweak unnecessary, though gains on integrated GPUs are expected to be minor ([NerdZap](https://nerdzap.com/news/steamos-3-8-28-steam-deck-what-changed/)).

The one sizeable hardware lever is variable rate shading (VRS), which shades some screen areas once per 2×2 block of pixels instead of once per pixel. Valve built a driver override for it, documented as testing-only and limited to this class of chip ([Mesa envvars](https://github.com/chaotic-cx/mesa-mirror/blob/bffd91947efc1932da99aca69fc540d6d4201b0c/docs/envvars.rst)). It gave 30%+ in some games and close to nothing in others ([Phoronix](https://www.phoronix.com/review/radeon-radv-vrs)). It also degrades edges and text, so it must be image-scored; set through a launch-option variable, it is Tier S. Diagnosis needs a third class beyond "CPU-bound" and "GPU-bound": "power-bound". That class is detectable because the GPU power reading on an APU includes the CPU, and the driver reports throttling ([Linux kernel docs](https://kernel.org/doc/html/v7.2/gpu/amdgpu/thermal.html)). Moving watts from CPU to GPU by disabling SMT (the second thread on each CPU core) or limiting CPU boost is a plausible lever for GPU-bound games, but no 2025–26 Deck measurement exists (inference). **Taken together, the silicon has single-digit percentages left. The real "last drops" are pixels the 7-inch screen cannot show and milliseconds lost to queues and limiters.**

## Cloud streaming beats local tricks for PS5-era games, if Mexico City latency holds

For PS5-era games, the strongest alternative is not a local technique at all. GeForce NOW (GFN) has a native Steam Deck app that streams at the Deck's own 1280×800. It ran at 60 fps at launch and is now listed at up to 90 fps in handheld mode, since NVIDIA's RTX 5080-class servers arrived ([PCGH](https://www.pcgameshardware.de/Steam-Deck-Hardware-277668/Specials/Geforce-Now-auf-dem-Handheld-PC-1473728/); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GeForce_Now)). Ultimate costs US$19.99 a month, or MXN 439 in Mexico; Performance costs US$9.99. Both have had a 100-hour monthly cap since January 2026 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GeForce_Now); [Xataka México](https://www.xataka.com.mx/videojuegos/nvidia-geforce-now-se-actualiza-ahora-podemos-contamos-rendimiento-rtx-5080-a-5k-120-fps-suscripcion-ultimate-mexico)). Wilds and Space Marine 2 are on the service ([Icy Veins](https://www.icy-veins.com/monster-hunter-wilds/news/monster-hunter-wilds-joins-geforce-now-hunt-anywhere); [Neowin](https://www.neowin.net/news/nvidia-geforce-now-adds-warhammer-40000-space-marine-ii-and-more-this-week/)); Helldivers 2 is not ([u4n](https://www.u4n.com/news/is-helldivers-2-on-ps4-game-pass-switch-geforce-now.html)). The game runs on a Windows server, so Space Marine 2's anti-cheat stops being a Deck problem (inference). Battery life roughly doubles. PCGH measured at most 7.1 W of whole-system draw and 7.5–9.5 hours of play ([PCGH](https://www.pcgameshardware.de/Steam-Deck-Hardware-277668/Specials/Geforce-Now-auf-dem-Handheld-PC-1473728/)). NoobFeed measured 7–8 W while streaming, against about 15 W for a game capped at 45 fps natively ([NoobFeed](https://www.noobfeed.com/hardware/nvidia-geforce-now-on-steam-deck-gameplay)).

Two unknowns decide whether GFN meets Fede's "native" bar. The first is latency. The published figures come from wired setups in Europe or the US: PCGH saw 12–14 ms on average, probably the app's network figure, and Tom's Guide measured 17 ms click-to-photon in a 360 fps esports test on a fast line ([PCGH](https://www.pcgameshardware.de/Steam-Deck-Hardware-277668/Specials/Geforce-Now-auf-dem-Handheld-PC-1473728/); [Tom's Guide](https://www.tomsguide.com/gaming/nvidia-geforce-now-with-rtx-5080-is-a-console-killer-i-played-5k-at-120-fps-and-it-blew-my-mind)). Mexico is served "directly from the United States", from an unnamed city ([Xataka México](https://www.xataka.com.mx/videojuegos/nvidia-geforce-now-se-actualiza-ahora-podemos-contamos-rendimiento-rtx-5080-a-5k-120-fps-suscripcion-ultimate-mexico)). Nobody has measured GFN on a Deck over Wi-Fi from Mexico City. The second unknown is image quality. GFN trades upscaler and frame-generation artifacts for video-compression artifacts. A Deck stream used about 12 Mbps ([NoobFeed](https://www.noobfeed.com/hardware/nvidia-geforce-now-on-steam-deck-gameplay)), and no source has measured how that looks on the 800p panel. Both unknowns are cheap to settle. An Ultimate day pass costs US$7.99 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GeForce_Now)), and DeckForge's own camera and image-quality harness could score a GFN session against a native reference, which no public source has done.

Local streaming from a gaming PC is the quality ceiling, but Fede owns no host. Wired 2021 tests of Steam Remote Play and Moonlight measured about 18–20 ms ([TechSpot](https://www.techspot.com/article/2198-steam-remote-play-vs-moonlight/)). There is no hour cap, any game works including anti-cheat titles, and the Apollo streaming host can render at exactly 1280×800 and 90 Hz for a Deck ([Apollo](https://github.com/ClassicOldSong/Apollo)). Valve's Steam Machine starts at US$1,049 and sold through a reservation queue ([Gfinity](https://www.gfinityesports.com/article/the-steam-machine-will-start-at-a-whopping-1049-wont-include-a-controller)), about five years of GFN Ultimate. A used RX 6700 XT averages about US$313 ([ResalePrices](https://resaleprices.com/gpu/amd-rx-6700-xt)), and a complete used-parts PC is estimated at US$600–900 (estimate). The Mac cannot host Wilds, Space Marine 2 or Helldivers 2, which are Windows games with anti-cheat (inference). Windows on the Deck has no performance case either: a Legion Go S ran about 24% faster on SteamOS than on Windows 11 ([Tom's Guide](https://www.tomsguide.com/gaming/handheld-gaming/new-benchmarks-reveal-steamos-beats-windows-on-handheld-gaming-performance-heres-the-results)). Xbox Cloud's top tier costs US$29.99 and is tied to the Game Pass catalog, not Fede's Steam library ([GSMArena](https://m.gsmarena.com/microsoft_announces_50_price_hike_for_xbox_game_pass_ultimate-news-69792.php)). Boosteroid (from €7.49) and Shadow (from €32.99) prices come only from an aggregator site of uncertain quality ([tech-insider](https://tech-insider.org/sv/shadow-pc-vs-geforce-now-vs-boosteroid-2026/)).

Lossless Scaling is a complement, not an alternative. On Linux it exists only as lsfg-vk frame generation, whose latency cost was covered above; its upscaler is not documented as available there. Running frame generation on a cloud or Moonlight stream would stack interpolation delay on top of network delay and likely magnify compression blocks (inference). GFN already runs frame generation on the server for supported games ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GeForce_Now)).

| Undervolt, scheduler, power tuning | +2–4% fps or better battery | Neutral | $0 | All | Knobs inside DeckForge, not a strategy |

The implication for DeckForge is a new verdict, not a pivot. When the best measured native configuration misses every rung of the 45/40/30 ladder and the game is on GFN, DeckForge should say so and suggest a streaming trial. That turns the Wilds problem from a failure into a correct answer. Where the floor sits is Fede's call: if 30 fps at 90 Hz counts as acceptable, Wilds gets a "30@90" verdict rather than a cloud suggestion; if anything under 40 should go to the cloud, Wilds is routed there.

## Once launched, DeckForge should edit launch strings and prefix files, not Steam's internals

Put together, the evidence describes a tool that mostly edits short text strings and per-user files, measures everything twice, and leaves Steam's internals alone. On launch it first identifies the game and its context:

- **The game's location:** the Steam app ID and which library holds it, since each library has its own Proton prefix ([GamingOnLinux guide](https://www.gamingonlinux.com/guides/view/how-to-find-where-steam-stores-proton-game-files/)).
- **Versions:** the game's build ID and the exact SteamOS, Proton and Mesa versions. Proton now bundles untagged development snapshots of DXVK and vkd3d-proton, so only the full build string identifies them ([Phoronix](https://phoronix.com/news/Proton-11.0-2)).
- **The Deck model:** the same TDP draws different real power on OLED and LCD ([willett.io](https://www.willett.io/posts/watts/)).
- **Anti-cheat status:** this fixes the game's tier.
- **The game's own Deck defaults:** these are the baseline to beat, because Steamworks SDK 1.65 lets developers ship hardware-specific defaults that Valve can update without a game patch ([Digital Citizen](https://www.digitalcitizen.life/steamworks-sdk-1-65-helps-developers-optimise-games-for-steam-deck-steam-machine-and-steam-frame/)).

It then diagnoses whether the game is CPU-, GPU- or power-bound before searching anything.

It then sorts every file it might touch into two classes, which matter more than the engine. **Per-user files inside the Proton prefix survive Steam's "verify files" and are the natural home for Tier S edits**, although the game's menu or a patch can still rewrite them. Examples are Unreal Engine's `Saved/Config` files (for Oblivion Remastered, under `.../compatdata/2623190/pfx/drive_c/users/steamuser/Documents/My Games/...` ([overkill.wtf](https://overkill.wtf/elder-scrolls-oblivion-remastered-best-settings-steam-deck))), Unity's settings in the prefix's Windows registry ([Unity docs](https://docs.unity3d.com/ScriptReference/PlayerPrefs.html)), and Space Marine 2's user folder. **Files in the install folder, such as Wilds' `config.ini`, Unity's `boot.config` and mod files, can be reverted by verify and updates**. For Wilds, a verify flagged two unnamed modified files; whether it restores `config.ini` is still to be tested ([Steam discussion](https://steamcommunity.com/app/2246340/discussions/0/596271270005369411)). An edited `boot.config` also failed Easy Anti-Cheat's check in one Unity game ([The Fun Pimps forums](https://community.thefunpimps.com/threads/gfx-enable-native-gfx-jobs-flag-performance-increase.18407)). **CLAUDE.md's Tier S definition ("in-game settings files") should be split to match: prefix files are Tier S, and install-folder files are Tier I unless proven safe for a specific game.** For Wilds, which QUALITY_TARGET.md lists as having no online anti-cheat, the risk is unquantified rather than absent. Its anti-tamper code reportedly interacts with REFramework, Capcom has said nothing ([Multiplayer.it](https://multiplayer.it/notizie/una-mod-migliora-la-fluidita-di-monster-hunter-wilds-su-pc-contrastando-la-tecnologia-anti-tamper-di-capcom.html)), and Steam verify flags modified files.

Most driver and translation-layer settings need no file at all. Every layer reads environment variables (settings handed to a program when it starts), and those can go in a game's Steam launch options. DXVK settings go through `DXVK_CONFIG` ([DXVK README](https://github.com/doitsujin/dxvk/blob/71ec7b5be6c517db39bcca4d38f38a63f6ddbc56/README.md)). vkd3d-proton flags go through `VKD3D_CONFIG`; Proton itself sometimes adds to this variable, so DeckForge must append rather than overwrite ([Proton source](https://github.com/ValveSoftware/Proton/blob/bc896217ffcd67f892f952b010f898fe7bd0f0a1/proton)). RADV options, and any Mesa driver option, can be set through an environment variable of the same name ([Mesa xmlconfig.c](https://github.com/chaotic-cx/mesa-mirror/blob/bffd91947efc1932da99aca69fc540d6d4201b0c/src/util/xmlconfig.c#L430-L448)). These settings are reversible, need no administrator access, and touch no system files.

Three things complicate applying them:

- **The launch-option text itself** lives in Steam's `localconfig.vdf`, which should only be written while Steam is closed ([steam-launchoptions script](https://fjordtek.com/git/Fincer/steam-launchoptions/blame/branch/master/steam_launchoptions.sh)). Existing Decky plugins sidestep this with a "copy launch option to clipboard" button ([decky-lsfg-vk](https://github.com/xXJSONDeruloXx/decky-lsfg-vk)).
- **Frame cap, refresh rate and scaling filter** are gamescope controls that any program can set, but Steam rewrites them, so the last writer wins ([gamescope source](https://github.com/ValveSoftware/gamescope); inference).
- **Steam Cloud** can silently revert a file. Valve tells developers not to sync graphics settings ([Steamworks](https://partner.steamgames.com/doc/steamhardware/recommendations)), but DeckForge should still snapshot each file before and after every launch.

Two habits from the modding community should be avoided. The first is making config files read-only, the common workaround, which silently freezes the in-game menu ([Nexus mod 183](https://www.nexusmods.com/monsterhunterwilds/mods/183)). DeckForge should instead fingerprint (hash) every file it writes in its R10 manifest, detect changes on each launch, and re-validate before re-applying. The second is trusting tuned profiles indefinitely. The 2025 Wilds Deck config became harmful after Capcom's patches, so every profile should carry the game's build ID and be re-measured when that changes.

Where things live also matters. Everything DeckForge installs must sit under `/home/deck`, because SteamOS's system partition is read-only and replaced on every update ([heguro notes](https://scrapbox.io/heguro/Steam_Deck%E3%81%AB%E3%81%A4%E3%81%84%E3%81%A6%E3%82%8BSteamOS_3%E7%92%B0%E5%A2%83%E3%81%AE%E4%BB%95%E6%A7%98)). A future DeckForge Vulkan layer can follow lsfg-vk's pattern: a small manifest file in `~/.local/share/vulkan/implicit_layer.d/`, switched on per game by a launch option ([decky-lsfg-vk](https://github.com/xXJSONDeruloXx/decky-lsfg-vk)). Decky, the plugin loader, broke after the April 2026 Steam client update ([PCGames.de](https://www.pcgames.de/Steam-Deck-Hardware-277668/News/Neues-Update-Decky-Loader-1529736/)). It should therefore be an optional front end, with the core running as a plain command-line tool.

The prior-art search explains both the opportunity and the risks. Every vendor "optimal settings" feature is a lookup table built offline, from NVIDIA's lab play-throughs to AMD's crowd-mined settings ([TechRadar](https://www.techradar.com/news/software/applications/in-the-pursuit-of-fps-how-amd-and-nvidia-are-tackling-pc-gaming-optimization-1210970/2)). AMD's current one-click bundle does not even support RDNA 2 ([AMD](https://www.amd.com/en/products/software/adrenalin/hypr-rx.html)). Valve now collects opt-in frame-rate data from Decks, but shows it only to developers, with at most a coarse relative setting level and render resolution rather than the individual settings that produced it ([GamingOnLinux](https://www.gamingonlinux.com/2026/10/valve-give-game-developers-more-steam-deck-framerate-data/page=1/)). **The research found no tool that measures a game on the user's own Deck, searches its settings, scores frame-time stability and image quality, and re-checks after updates. That closed loop is DeckForge's niche.** A targeted GitHub search and a check of Handheld Daemon and Handheld Companion are still open.

Temporal quality metrics now exist to score it. ColorVideoVDP accounts for artifacts that change over time and runs on an ordinary CPU as well as on NVIDIA GPUs ([ColorVideoVDP](https://github.com/gfxdisp/ColorVideoVDP)); that matters because Fede's analysis machine is a Mac. Intel's CGVQM was calibrated on supersampling, frame-interpolation and VRS distortions ([Intel](https://www.intel.com/content/www/us/en/developer/articles/technical/cgvqm-d-computer-graphics-video-quality.html)). The trust lessons are blunt. Samsung's hidden game throttling ended in a court-ordered payout ([SamMobile](https://www.sammobile.com/news/gos-controversy-ends-samsung-ordered-to-pay-galaxy-s22-users/)). AMD's Anti-Lag+ earned Counter-Strike 2 players VAC bans because it hooked game code ([Guru3D](https://www.guru3d.com/story/amd-in-counterstrike-2-can-result-in-vac-ban)). lsfg-vk's author left GitHub after finding over 87% of forks were AI-generated ([GamingOnLinux](https://www.gamingonlinux.com/2026/08/lossless-scaling-frame-generation-on-linux-lsfg-vk-moves-away-from-github-changes-license-due-to-ai-slop-forks/)). An AI-built tool will be met with suspicion, and the defense is what CLAUDE.md already requires: visible, reversible changes and an evidence bundle behind every recommendation.

## Conclusion

The overnight evidence moves DeckForge's center of gravity from frame rate to latency and evidence. On PS5-era games, frame rate is mostly fixed by the hardware and the developer. How the limiter, the render queue and the upscaler behave is set by the user, and that is where a safe-tier tool can make a measurable difference in every game, anti-cheat titles included. The biggest of those levers, Steam's limiter at 45 fps on a 90 Hz panel, has no public measurement newer than 2022. That means DeckForge's first publishable result could be a number nobody else has: the button-to-screen cost of each way to lock 45 fps on a current OLED. The next two measurements follow directly. One is FSR 4 INT8's real per-frame cost on the Deck, read from GPU timestamps, including whether the BC-250 integer optimizations carry over. The other is a GeForce NOW day pass from Mexico City, scored with the same camera and image-quality harness. Alongside them, the project needs one test game it can actually win.

The product that falls out of this is narrower and more honest than the original pitch. It wins on games that waste the hardware and says "BLOCKED, here is why, try streaming" on games that outgrow it. It treats frame generation as a labeled motion-smoothing option, with its latency cost printed beside it. The risk to watch is that others absorb pieces of the pitch: AMD's lighter handheld FSR 4 and Valve's crowd frame-rate data could each take one piece within a year. Neither closes the measure-search-verify loop, and that loop is where DeckForge's durable value sits.
